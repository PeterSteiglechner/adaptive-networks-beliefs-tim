## Abstract

People's beliefs about social or political issues can change in different ways, even when they encounter the same new information. Some people show compliance through lasting belief change, others show resilience through temporary belief change, and yet others show resistance by not changing their beliefs at all. Existing explanations often focus on belief content, evidence, motivation, personality, or social influence. We introduce Adaptive Networks of Beliefs, a theory describing how beliefs and belief relations co-evolve through internal and social adaptation. Internal adaptation changes belief relations based on the individual's belief changes. Social adaptation aligns belief relations across individuals. Simulations show that internal adaptation generates heterogeneous belief-network structures, causing individuals with similar targeted beliefs to show compliance, resilience, or resistance under the same external pressure. These response patterns depend on how the targeted belief is embedded in the belief network before the pressure is applied. Compliance is associated with less balanced, less clustered, and more dissonant belief networks. Resistance is associated with stronger connectedness and balance around the targeted belief. Social adaptation reduces belief-network heterogeneity but changes response patterns non-monotonically. Weak to moderate social adaptation increases compliance, whereas stronger social adaptation increases resilience. Finally, external pressure affects not only the targeted belief but also how it is embedded, and this affects future belief change. Our results illustrate how individual differences in belief change can arise from adaptive belief-network structures itself. Explaining why people's beliefs show compliance, resilience, or resistance therefore requires understanding how these networks change through internal and social adaptation.

## Introduction

People's beliefs about social or political issues or consumer products often respond differently to new information, whether it comes in the form of a scientific finding, a statement from a political candidate, or a  promotional message from a company (Chan et al., 2017; Walter &amp; Tukachinsky, 2020; Costello et al., 2024;  Kahan et al., 2012; Toomey, 2023; Vlasceanu et al., 2024). Some beliefs align with the new information and remain changed. Other beliefs change temporarily and return to their previous state once the informational  pressure ceases. Yet others resist change altogether. Similarly, people differ in how they respond to the same  information, with some adjusting their beliefs, others making temporary adjustments, and others resisting  change. Why are some beliefs and individual belief systems more compliant, while others are more resilient or  resistant? 

Individual differences in belief persistence have been linked to variations in initial support for a belief and the strength of evidence (Vlasceanu et al., 2024; Acemoglu &amp; Ozdaglar, 2011; Sunstein et al., 2017), uncertainty and subjectivity of the belief (Oktar &amp; Lombrozo, 2025), social influence (Oktar &amp; Lombrozo, 2025; Cialdini &amp; Goldstein, 2004; Scheufele, 2013), motivated reasoning (Kahan, 2016; Druckman &amp; McGrath, 2019), emotional involvement (Ecker et al., 2022), and stable personality traits such as stubbornness or openness to experience (Mobilia et al., 2007; Matz, 2021). These explanations often focus on factors that promote or hinder belief change in isolation. This does not mean that they are necessarily simple or limited to single beliefs, but that they usually explain belief persistence by identifying one factor, or one class of factors, as especially important. A more systemic explanation asks how belief change depends on the larger system of cognitions in which a belief is embedded, including personal beliefs about related issues and beliefs about what relevant social actors think. Recent research on belief systems (Bizyaeva et al., 2025; Brandt &amp; Sleegers, 2021; Dalege et al., 2016, 2018, 2017, 2025; Friedkin et al., 2016; Goldberg &amp; Stein, 2018; Vlasceanu et al., 2021) suggests that differences in belief persistence across issues and individuals might depend on the overall structure of belief systems and the way different beliefs are related to each other. However, we still lack a clear understanding of how belief systems change through internal and social processes, and how the dynamic structure of these systems shapes the propensity for further belief change. Here, we provide a computational theory that represents these systems as dynamic networks of beliefs embedded in social contexts. In this theory, not only beliefs, but also belief relations change over time due to dissonances arising within the network, between individuals, and between the network and external information. Because these belief relations adapt, individuals with similar beliefs can develop different belief network structures. These structures then provide different constraints and opportunities for change in response to the same external informational pressure, leading some beliefs and individuals to become compliant, others resilient, and others resistant. In what follows, we introduce belief network approaches and then describe and formalize the Adaptive Networks of Beliefs (ANB) theory. ANB builds on Networks of Beliefs theory (Dalege et al., 2025) and extends it by allowing belief network structures to adapt over time through internal adaptation and social adaptation. Using simulations, we first examine how internal adaptation can make individual belief networks grow apart, and how this heterogeneity in belief-network structure creates different responses to the same external pressure: compliance, resilience, or resistance. We show that these response patterns are associated with characteristic belief-network structures: compliance, compared to resilience and resistance, is associated with lower balance and lower clustering in the belief network; resistance, compared to resilience, is associated especially with higher connectedness and balance around the focal belief, that is, the belief targeted by external pressure and exchanged socially in the model. We further show that external pressure changes not only the focal belief, but also how this belief is embedded in the belief network, suggesting downstream consequences for future belief change. Finally, we show that social adaptation can either increase compliance or support resilience, depending on how strongly belief network structures converge within social neighborhoods. Overall, the results show how individual differences in belief change can arise from adaptive belief-network structure itself, without invoking differences in the specific content of beliefs, motivation, or personality.

## Networks of Beliefs

One way to describe individual belief systems more directly is through belief networks. The belief network approaches mentioned above treat beliefs-from assumptions about states of the world to opinions on moral and political issues (Olsson &amp; Galesic, 2024)-as nodes and belief relations as edges in a network of interconnected beliefs that mutually reinforce, constrain, and stabilize one another. A recent Networks of Beliefs (NB) theory assumes that belief updating is driven by dissonance (Dalege et al., 2025). Individuals can feel dissonance about a belief when it conflicts with other beliefs that are related to it in the belief network. For example, if concern about climate change and concern about environmental destruction are positively related in a person's belief network, it feels dissonant to be only concerned about one but not the other. Relations can also be negative. A person might support electric vehicles as aligned with concern about climate change but in conflict with other environmental concerns, thus constituting an imbalance in the network that requires some compromise in beliefs. In NB theory, individuals can also feel social dissonance when their personal beliefs are misaligned with their social beliefs, that is, beliefs about what their social contacts believe. For example, being highly concerned about climate change while believing that one's social contacts are dismissive creates social dissonance[^1]. NB theory assumes that individuals generally aim to reduce these forms of dissonance (Dalege et al., 2025; Festinger, 1954; Thagard &amp; Verbeurgt, 1998) and formalizes how individuals update their beliefs following a probabilistic updating rule inspired by statistical physics. In ANB, we keep this dissonance-reduction logic but simplify the parts of NB theory that involve social beliefs: we do not represent social beliefs as separate nodes and assume that agents observe the 'true' focal beliefs of their social contacts. In ANB, external pressure provides another source of dissonance for the focal belief when this belief is misaligned with a pressure toward a particular position, for example, a political campaign about climate change mitigation, or discounts for electric vehicles. This network framing can help explain why some belief systems are more persistent than others (Scheffer et al., 2022) and why some beliefs can be more difficult to change than others (Brandt &amp; Vallabha, 2023; Turner-Zwinkels &amp; Brandt, 2022). However, most belief network approaches assume that, unlike beliefs, belief relations are static and homogeneous across individuals, with few exceptions (Brandt, 2022; Smal et al., 2026). This implies that individuals who hold similar beliefs should experience the same dissonance in response to external information, and update their beliefs in a similar way. Heterogeneous responses to the same external pressure can only emerge from noise in the probabilistic belief updating or from differences in social influence, but not from differences in belief systems. In contrast, here we assume that belief relations may adapt as individuals update their beliefs and interact with others. As a result, individuals can experience different patterns of dissonance and show different patterns of belief change and persistence, even when they hold similar beliefs and are embedded in similar social contexts.

[^1]: Full NB theory further distinguishes external dissonance, which arises when social beliefs do not correspond to others' actual beliefs. We do not discuss this type of dissonance here.

## Adaptive Networks of Beliefs

Adaptive Networks of Beliefs (ANB) is a theory of belief dynamics in which both beliefs and belief relations co-evolve over time, leading to individual differences in belief systems. ANB extends NB theory by allowing individuals' belief relations to change, in line with the many documented examples of such changes, for example in domains of cultural practices (DellaPosta et al., 2015), climate change beliefs (Dunlap et al., 2016), support for abortion (Williams, 2011), and support for childhood vaccination (Goldberg &amp; Stein, 2018). In ANB, belief relations can change in two ways. One is internal adaptation, where consistent changes of two beliefs strengthen and inconsistent changes weaken the relation between two beliefs. The other is social adaptation, where the structure of an individual's belief network becomes more similar to the structure of belief networks of their social contacts. The way we model the process of internal adaptation is inspired by associative learning, in particular Hebbian learning (Hebb, 1949), describing how connections become stronger when elements are co-activated. In ANB, consistent belief change strengthens the relation between two beliefs. If two beliefs move in the same direction and their relation is positive, or if two beliefs move in opposite directions and their relation is negative, the relation between these beliefs becomes stronger. Inconsistent belief change weakens the relation between two beliefs. If two beliefs move in the same direction, although their relation is negative, or if two beliefs move in opposite directions, although their relation is positive, then their relation becomes weaker. For example, if a person perceives concerns about climate change and environment to be related and becomes more concerned about both, this relation becomes even stronger. Internal adaptation therefore ties belief relations to the individual's own belief changes. We model the process of social adaptation as local convergence of belief networks within social neighborhoods. This assumption builds on work on social learning and opinion dynamics showing that beliefs and opinions are shaped through observation, communication, media, and other sources of information within social contexts (Acemoglu &amp; Ozdaglar, 2011). Social influence can also shape relations among beliefs. Work on associative diffusion shows how social interaction can transmit associations between cultural elements, so that people come to connect issues, practices, identities, and attitudes that need not be intrinsically related (Goldberg &amp; Stein, 2018). Related work on lifestyle politics shows that homophily and social influence can produce alignment between political ideology and otherwise weakly related tastes and practices (DellaPosta et al., 2015). Experiments on collective belief synchronization further show that communication within social networks can produce convergence in shared belief structures, not only agreement on isolated beliefs (Vlasceanu et al., 2021).

In ANB, social contacts can therefore shape not only which beliefs individuals hold, but also how these beliefs reinforce or weaken each other. For example, when social contacts do not consider environmental and climate change concerns to be related, it is difficult to maintain a strong relation between these issues. Conversely, when a social neighborhood treats these concerns as closely related, this relation can become part of a shared belief network structure. Social adaptation therefore constrains idiosyncratic belief networks and can produce shared relations between beliefs within social circles. ANB is related to the recent Learning Ising Attitude Model (LIAM; Smal et al. (2026)), which extends the network theory of attitudes (Dalege et al., 2016, 2018) by adding Hebbian learning to explain how balanced attitude networks can emerge and remain stable. Like ANB, LIAM treats network relations as adaptive, but the learning mechanism differs: LIAM updates relations based on covariation in node states, whereas ANB updates relations based on whether beliefs change consistently or inconsistently. We return to a fuller comparison with LIAM in the General Discussion. In sum, in ANB belief updating changes the values of beliefs in response to dissonance. This includes personal dissonance within the individual's belief network and, for the focal belief, social dissonance with the observed focal beliefs of social contacts. When external pressure is present, it creates an additional source of dissonance for the focal belief if the focal belief is misaligned with the pressure. Belief network updating changes the relations among beliefs. In particular, internal adaptation changes relations based on the individual's own belief changes. Social adaptation changes relations based on the belief-network structures of social contacts. ANB therefore relaxes two simplifying assumptions often made in belief-network models: that belief relations are fixed during belief updating (e.g., Dalege et al., 2025), and that belief-network structures are often specified in the same way across individuals. In ANB, belief-network structures are idiosyncratic and adaptive individuallevel properties, but are also constrained by social contexts. These extensions change how external pressure is expected to affect belief change. If individuals differ in the strength and direction of the relations among beliefs, they can evaluate the same belief and the same external pressure differently. External pressure that changes one individual's belief may fail to change another individual's belief, or may change it only temporarily. Next, we describe how we implement ANB theory in a computational agent-based model that can be used to simulate various belief trajectories and responses to external pressures.

## Model Description

In ANB, each agent has multiple beliefs embedded in an individual belief network (Figure 1). The nodes of this network are beliefs, and the edges represent how the agent relates these beliefs to each other. Agents update their beliefs in response to dissonance with their own belief network and with the beliefs of their social contacts if they exchange these beliefs during social interaction. This belief updating follows NB theory (Dalege et al., 2025). ANB extends NB theory by allowing the belief-network structure itself to change over time. Internal adaptation changes relations among beliefs as a function of the agent's own belief changes. Social adaptation moves relations among beliefs toward the belief-network structures of social contacts. Following NB theory, we distinguish one belief as the focal belief. This is not because the other beliefs are irrelevant, but because the model follows the dynamics of one issue through a broader belief system and social environment. The focal belief is the belief whose change we track, the belief observed by social contacts, and the belief targeted by external pressure. The other beliefs provide the belief-network context in which this issue is interpreted and updated. This allows us to examine how the same pressure on the same issue can lead to lasting belief change, temporary belief change, or resistance, depending on how that issue is embedded in the belief network.

## Beliefs and Belief Networks

We define a belief as the degree of approval or disapproval with a belief statement about some issue. Each agent has a set of $M$ beliefs $X = \{x_1, \ldots, x_M\}$, where each belief can take any value on a discretized scale from $-1$ to $1$, e.g. $x_m \in \mathcal{X} = \{-1, -0.9, -0.8, \ldots, 1\}$. Formally, one of the beliefs is the focal belief, denoted $x_{foc}$. This is the belief that is exchanged socially and targeted by external pressure. For example, even if individuals might have multiple interrelated beliefs about the economic, environmental, or social aspects of some political issue, they may end up discussing only the support for a particular related policy.

Agents in ANB are embedded in a social network. Agent $A$ has a set of social contacts $\mathcal{K}^{(A)}$. We assume that agent $A$ observes the focal beliefs of these social contacts[^2], $x_{foc}^{(k)}$ for $k \in \mathcal{K}^{(A)}$, that is, agent $A$ observes $x_{foc}^{(k)}$ of its contacts $k \in \mathcal{K}^{(A)}$, as well as their belief network structures (see below). The beliefs on (non-focal) issues, $x_{m \neq foc}$, are private beliefs of the agent and are not observable by others.

Beliefs are embedded in an individual-level belief network. In this network, nodes are beliefs and edges represent belief relations. The relation between any two beliefs, $m$ and $n$, in agent $A$'s belief network is represented by an edge weight $\omega_{mn}$, which captures the strength and direction of the relation and can take any positive or negative value.[^3] For example, the focal belief $foc$ is connected to the $M-1$ non-focal beliefs with weights $\omega_{foc,1}, \omega_{foc,2}, \ldots$. The belief system of agent $A$ thus consists of two elements: beliefs $X^{(A)}$ and belief-network structure $\Omega^{(A)}$.

## Dissonance and Belief Updating

A set of beliefs can feel consonant or dissonant to an agent. Agent $A$ feels personal dissonance when its beliefs, $X^{(A)}$, are not aligned with its belief network structure, $\Omega^{(A)}$. For example, if two beliefs are positively related in the agent's belief network, it is dissonant to hold one belief positively and the other negatively. In ANB, social dissonance arises when the focal belief, $x_{foc}^{(A)}$, of agent $A$ is not aligned with the observed focal beliefs of its social contacts, $x_{foc}^{(k)}$ for $k \in \mathcal{K}^{(A)}$. Combining personal and social dissonance, the dissonance of agent $A$ is

$$
D\left(X^{(A)} \mid \Omega^{(A)}\right) = -\underbrace{\sum_{m=2}^{M} \sum_{n=1}^{m-1} \omega_{mn}^{(A)} \cdot x_m^{(A)} \cdot x_n^{(A)}}_{\text{personal dissonance}} \; - \underbrace{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}^{(A)}} \rho \cdot x_{foc}^{(A)} \cdot x_{foc}^{(k)}}_{\text{social dissonance}} \,, \tag{1}
$$

where $\rho$ represents the weight of the (social) connection between the agent's own and one of its contact's focal belief. Alignment is represented by a multiplicative term, $x_m \cdot x_n$. Other definitions of alignment, such as distance-based terms $|x_m - x_n|$, are discussed in Dalege et al. (2025). With the multiplicative specification, aligned extreme beliefs produce lower dissonance than aligned moderate beliefs. In analogy to statistical physics, $D$ corresponds to the energy in a configuration of coupled magnetic dipoles (Olsson &amp; Galesic, 2024).

[^2]: We consider here a simplified version of NB theory wherein agents have perfectly accurate perceptions of others' beliefs.

[^3]: In the original NB theory, edge weights and attention to dissonance are distinct. Here, because we focus on idiosyncratic belief-network structures, we represent the dissonance-relevant relation between two beliefs with a single edge weight.

Agents update their beliefs to reduce personal and social dissonance. For each belief $x_m^{(A)}$, agent $A$ considers a possible new belief value $x_m'$. The probability of adopting this value depends on the change in dissonance that would result from replacing $x_m^{(A)}$ with $x_m'$:

$$
p\left(x_m^{(A)} \rightarrow x_m'\right) \sim \frac{1}{1 + e^{\beta \cdot \left[ D\left(X' \mid \Omega^{(A)}\right) - D\left(X^{(A)} \mid \Omega^{(A)}\right) \right]}} \,, \tag{2}
$$

where $X^{(A)}$ is the set of beliefs before the update, and $X'$ is identical to $X^{(A)}$ except that $x_m^{(A)}$ is replaced by $x_m'$. The parameter $\beta$ captures attention to dissonance.[^4] When $\beta$ is larger, belief updating is more strongly shaped by dissonance reduction. When $\beta$ is smaller, agents are more likely to adopt belief values that do not reduce dissonance. In one time step, agents potentially update each of their $M$ beliefs in random order. On average, this updating process moves agents toward belief configurations that are more aligned with their belief network structures and social environments.

## Belief Network Updating

Agents change their belief-network structure through two processes: internal adaptation and social adaptation. Internal adaptation changes relations among beliefs based on the agent's own belief changes. Social adaptation represents local convergence of belief networks within social neighborhoods.

First, we formalize internal adaptation as a process similar to Hebbian learning in a network of neurons. Consider the change in agent $A$'s beliefs $x_m$ and $x_n$ over time. For readability, we drop the agent superscript. We define the recent change in belief $x_m$ at time $t$ as the average change over the $\tau$ most recent time steps,

$$
\partial x_m(t) = \frac{1}{\tau} \sum_{i=0}^{\tau-1} \left[ x_m(t-i) - x_m(t-i-1) \right] .
$$

We call $\tau$ the activation memory because it captures how long agents recall changes in their own beliefs when they update their belief-network edge weights. In the simulations below, we use $\tau = 1$, so that $\partial x_m = x_m(t) - x_m(t-1)$. If agent $A$'s beliefs on issues $m$ and $n$ tend to move in the same direction (more approval for both $m$ and $n$ or less approval for both $m$ and $n$), the weight of the edge between $m$ and $n$, $\omega_{mn}$, increases. If they move in opposite directions, the edge weight decreases. A negative edge weight can thus become more negative if the two beliefs diverge further-that is, if they update in a way that is consistent with the negative edge.

Second, we formalize social adaptation as a convergence process. Agents observe the edge weights between beliefs in the belief networks of their social contacts and move toward their contacts' average belief network. This implies that the agents have some sense of how much their own belief relations deviate from the average belief relations in their social contacts' belief networks. For example, if an individual's social contacts connect electric vehicles with reducing their carbon footprint, the positive relation between climate change concern and support for electric vehicles can become stronger in the individual's own belief network as well.

Combining internal and social adaptation, the edge weight between beliefs $m$ and $n$ updates as:

$$
\omega_{mn}^{(A)} \leftarrow \omega_{mn}^{(A)} + \underbrace{\epsilon \cdot \partial x_m^{(A)} \cdot \partial x_n^{(A)}}_{\text{internal adaptation}} + \underbrace{\mu \cdot \left( \frac{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}^{(A)}} \omega_{mn}^{(k)}}{\left|\mathcal{K}^{(A)}\right|} - \omega_{mn}^{(A)} \right)}_{\text{social adaptation}} \,, \tag{3}
$$

where $\epsilon$ describes how strongly agents adapt belief relations in response to their own belief changes and $\mu$ describes how strongly agents converge to the average belief networks of their social contacts. Setting $\epsilon = 0$ removes internal adaptation. Setting $\mu = 0$ removes social adaptation.

[^4]: Here, $\beta$ captures the overall attention that agents pay to dissonance. We simplify NB theory by not distinguishing separate attention parameters for different sources of dissonance.

Internal adaptation in equation 3 is inspired by Hebbian learning, commonly summarized as "fire together, wire together" (Hebb, 1949), and by related formal approaches to relational learning (Monroe &amp; Read, 2008). In ANB, beliefs that change together become more strongly related. Our formalization follows the core aspects of Hebbian learning from Gerstner and Kistler (2002), namely locality, cooperativity, and synaptic depression. It does not impose bounded edge weights (here, agents can feel any degree of dissonance between two beliefs, at least if $\mu = 0$), competition between edge weights (strengthening one belief relation does not weaken others), or stability phases (agents' capacity to adapt their belief network structures does not vanish). Agents can therefore differ in their overall connectedness, and their belief network structures can continue to adapt over time. In combination with the dissonance computation (equation 1) and belief updating (equation 2), this means that beliefs that change together will tend to continue to change together.

Internal adaptation can either reduce or temporarily increase dissonance, depending on whether beliefs are already aligned with the belief network. If an agent's beliefs are aligned with the belief network, both consistent or inconsistent belief updating and the subsequent strengthening or weakening of belief network edges contribute to reduce the dissonance of an agent (see detailed example in Appendix A). However, when the agent's beliefs are not aligned with the belief network, it can temporarily increase the agent's dissonance. Assume, for example, Alice has two beliefs $x_1 = -0.7$ and $x_2 = 0.3$, although the two beliefs are positively related in her belief network, $\omega_{12} = 1$. When her beliefs change in a network-consistent way (that is, they move in the same direction, for example to $x_1 = -0.5$ and $x_2 = 0.5$), internal adaptation strengthens the relation between these two beliefs. However, this strengthening increases Alice's dissonance from $D = -\omega_{12} \cdot x_1 \cdot x_2 = -1 \cdot (-0.7) \cdot 0.3 = 0.21$ to $D_{\text{static edge}} = -1 \cdot (-0.5) \cdot 0.5 = 0.25$ vs. $D_{\text{adaptive edge}} = -(1 + 0.2 \cdot 0.2) \cdot (-0.5) \cdot 0.5 \approx 0.26$ (with $\epsilon = 1$ and $\mu = 0$). If Alice pays sufficient attention to dissonance, $\beta > 0$, she will eventually align the two beliefs. Once the sign of the beliefs are aligned with their edge, strengthening the edge weights contributes to reducing her dissonance.

## External pressure

We use the adaptive belief network model to simulate individual responses to external pressure on the focal belief. External pressure can represent a promotional campaign, a game-changing external event, or another piece of information that is relevant to the focal belief. In the simulations below, this pressure is temporary and favors more positive values of the focal belief. As a result, it reduces dissonance for focal beliefs that are aligned with the pressure and increases dissonance for focal beliefs that are misaligned with it. Specifically, this pressure adds a term to the dissonance function for all agents $A$ for a specific time period

$$
D\left(X^{(A)} \mid \Omega^{(A)}\right)^{\text{with pressure}} = \left[\text{equation 1}\right] - s \cdot x_{foc}^{(A)} \,. \tag{4}
$$

The parameter $s$ is the strength of the external pressure. In the statistical physics analogy, this represents an external field on a single node. In belief network language, it represents an additional node added to each agent's belief network with value +1 connected with edge weight $s$ to the focal belief (see also the sketch in Figure 1). The model is in principle flexible to include external pressures acting on non-focal beliefs (replace $s \cdot x_{foc}$ with $s \cdot x_m$ for some $m$) or on edge weights (add the term in equation 3), but we do not discuss these types of pressures here. To simulate how agents respond to external pressures, we simulate a warm-up period with $s = 0$ for time steps $t = 0$ to $100$, followed by an external pressure event with $s > 0$ between $t = 101$ and $150$. We evaluate the agents' focal beliefs before the external pressure event (between t = 91 and 100), during it (between t = 141 and 150), and some time after the event (between t = 191 and 200). To avoid that random fluctuations determine the responses, we averaged each agent's focal belief over the ten time steps before, during, and after the event.

## Simulation Results

## Model Setup and Choices

We use the simulations to examine how adaptive belief network structures generate heterogeneity across agents, and how this heterogeneity affects (i) the dynamics of focal beliefs and (ii) differences in individual response patterns to external pressure. The simulation setup is the following: We consider N = 100 agents embedded in an Erdős-Rényi random social network with link probability p = 0.1, such that the agents have on average approximately 10 contacts. The connections between the agents are fixed throughout the simulation.

Each agent is initialized with M = 10 beliefs, which we randomly draw from the belief space xm ∈ X = {-1,-0.9,-0.8, . . . , 1}. We create the belief network as a fully connected network between these M beliefs and initialize the edge weights with ωmn = ω0 = 0.2. In each time step, the agent updates all M beliefs (equation 2) in random order and all belief-network edges (equation 3), where internal adaptation is controlled by parameter ϵ and social adaptation by µ. Agent updating is asynchronous and in random order. Table 1 summarizes all parameters and our baseline configuration. We present a sensitivity analysis where we vary each of the parameters in Appendix E.

In the following, we first investigate the model with internal adaptation of belief networks ϵ &gt; 0 and social adaptation set to µ = 0. This allows us to isolate how internal adaptation generates heterogeneity in belief network structures across agents. We then compare responses to external pressure across four cases: static belief networks with low edge weights, static belief networks with high edge weights, belief networks that adapt until the pressure event and remain fixed thereafter, and belief networks that continue to adapt throughout the simulation. We next characterize agents with different response patterns by comparing their beliefs, social embeddedness, belief network structures, and dissonance before the pressure event. We use these comparisons and perform regression analyzes to assess which pre-pressure properties are associated with compliance, resistance, and resilience. Finally, we investigate how social adaptation, µ &gt; 0, affects the individuals' belief network heterogeneity and their belief responses to external pressures.

## Belief Dynamics on Static and Adaptive Belief Networks

When belief network structures remain static throughout a simulation, agents update their beliefs on fixed belief networks, making the model close to NB theory (Dalege et al., 2025). With the baseline configuration and low edge weight ω0 = 0.2, the 100 agents always reach consensus about the focal belief in all simulations (standard deviation of focal beliefs, σfoc, static-ω=0.2 = 0.16 ± 0.04). With high edge weight ω0 = 0.8, the focal beliefs polarize such that around half of the agents end up with strongly negative and the other half with strongly positive focal beliefs (σfoc, static-ω=0.8 = 0.97 ± 0.01). This resembles the results from Dalege et al. (2025).

The belief network of agent 61 is coherently structured and balanced, such that beliefs with equal signs tend to be positively related, while beliefs with opposite signs tend to be negatively related. This is an example of a low-dissonance belief system in which the belief content fits to the belief network structure. The belief network of agent 79 is less structured and balanced, providing an example of a high-dissonance belief system.

## Responses to External Pressure

Individual responses to external pressure depend on how the focal belief is embedded in the agent's belief network after these belief networks have adapted in different ways and thus become heterogeneous. We induce an external pressure event with pressure strength s from t = 101 to t = 150, which increases the dissonance for agents with a negative focal belief (' misaligned' belief) and reduces it for agents with a positive focal belief (' aligned' belief; see equation 4). Figure 3A shows an example simulation with external pressure s = 4 (see Appendix Figure A3 for the same simulation with weaker or stronger external pressure) and illustrates the major response patterns. Here, we focus on agents with negative, i.e. misaligned, focal beliefs at t = 100. We distinguish three general response patterns: first, an agent is compliant when its negative belief before the event aligns with the positive pressure and remains positive after the event, such that the external pressure induces lasting belief change. Second, an agent is resistant when its focal belief remains negative throughout the simulation, such that the pressure fails to produce alignment with the pressure. Third, an agent is resilient when its focal belief temporarily complies with the pressure but returns to a negative value after the event, such that the pressure induces temporary, but non-lasting belief change. A theoretical fourth, but negligible, response pattern is late compliance, where the focal belief remains negative during the event but becomes positive after the event.

Figures 3B-E show the frequency of response patterns in 100 simulations with static and adaptive belief networks as a function of external pressure strength s. In simulations with static belief networks, agents with misaligned beliefs prior to the pressure event either show full compliance when the belief-network edges are weak (ω0 = 0.2, panel B) or show resistance or resilience when the belief-network edges are strong (ω0 = 0.8, panel C). However, in simulations in which belief networks adapt either until the pressure event (panel D) or throughout the whole simulation (panel E), agents exhibit diverse response patterns. This co-existence of response patterns is due to the heterogeneity of belief networks before the pressure event and is largely independent of whether belief networks continue to adapt after the pressure event begins (panels D vs. E). The stronger the pressure, the fewer agents remain resistant. However, at the same time the proportion of compliant agents saturates, and resilience dominates, even under very strong external pressure.

## Properties of Compliant, Resilient, and Resistant Agents

To investigate how agents with different response patterns differ, we examine their belief-system properties just before the pressure event. We focus on the pre-pressure period because responses to external pressure should depend on how the focal belief is embedded before the pressure is applied. If adaptive belief-network structures shape responses to external pressure, then compliance, resistance, and resilience should be associated with differences in belief extremity, social embeddedness, belief-network structure, and dissonance before the pressure event. We define metrics for these properties in Table 2. The metrics capture (i) belief extremity for the focal and the average non-focal beliefs, (ii) social embeddedness, via the number of social contacts, (iii) belief-network structure, via connectedness (or average node strength), degree of balance (Cartwright &amp; Harary, 1956), and clustering (Onnela et al., 2005), (iv) embedding of the focal belief in the belief network, via connectedness, balance, and betweenness centrality of the focal belief, and (v) dissonance involving the focal belief, non-focal beliefs, and focal beliefs of social contacts. We report the metrics for s = 4 for the different response patterns in Table 3; corresponding results for simulations with other pressure strengths can be found in Appendix Tables A1-A4.

## Social Adaptation

The results so far show that internal adaptation can generate heterogeneous belief networks and heterogeneous responses to external pressure. We now ask how these results change when belief networks also converge locally through social adaptation. To do this, we vary µ and examine how social adaptation affects belief-network heterogeneity, belief-network structure, and responses to external pressure.

Social adaptation makes belief networks more similar across agents. As µ increases, agents' belief-network heterogeneity decreases, measured as the average Frobenius distance between each pair of belief networks (Figure 6C). The example belief networks in Figures 6A and 6B illustrate this change for weak social adaptation, µ = 0.005, and stronger social adaptation, µ = 0.2. The same edge varies less across agents when social adaptation is stronger and its strength tends to become less extreme (see also Appendix Figure A7).

Response patterns change non-monotonically with social adaptation. For weak to moderate social adaptation, more social adaptation leads to less resistance, less resilience, and more compliance with the external pressure (Figure 6D with pressure strength s = 4). In this range, social adaptation reduces heterogeneity across belief networks and lowers the average belief-network connectedness. This makes the focal belief less strongly embedded in the belief network and therefore more likely to remain aligned with the direction of the pressure after the pressure is removed. When µ exceeds a threshold, here around µ &gt; 0.02, this pattern changes. Belief-network heterogeneity drops significantly while clustering and balance around the focal belief increase.

## General Discussion

Our results show that internal adaptation can generate heterogeneous belief network structures, and that this heterogeneity shapes responses to external pressure. When belief-network structures are static, individuals tend to either comply with the external pressure when the belief relations are weak, or resist belief change (at least in the long term) when belief relations are strong. All individuals with a given focal belief targeted by the same external pressure will respond in the same way. That is, there will be no individual differences. In contrast, when belief relations can adapt in individual belief networks, individuals develop different beliefnetwork structures even when they start from the same initial network and hold similar focal beliefs. As a result, within a single simulation, the same external pressure can lead individuals with the same focal beliefs to quite different response patterns: compliance, resilience, or resistance. Responses depend on how the focal belief is embedded in an individual's belief network before the external pressure is applied. Compared with resilience and resistance, compliance is associated with less balanced, less clustered, and more dissonant belief networks. Compared with resilience, resistance is associated especially with stronger connectedness and balance around the focal belief. We further show that social adaptation affects both belief-network heterogeneity and response patterns. As individuals' social contacts influence do not only beliefs but also belief relations, individual belief networks become more similar across individuals, and edges become less extreme. Weak to moderate levels of social adaptation reduce resilience and resistance and increase compliance, because the focal belief tends to be less strongly embedded in the individuals' belief networks. Stronger levels of social adaptation, however, can increase balance and clustering around the focal belief, which reduces compliance and increases resilience of a misaligned belief to external pressure. Taken together, the model illustrates how adaptive belief networks create different pathways to belief change and persistence. Internal adaptation produces heterogeneous belief network structures, which produce heterogeneous response patterns. Social adaptation reduces this heterogeneity, but can also create shared network structures that support belief resilience rather than lasting compliance to external information. Our results place ANB within a broader view of belief change in which individuals do not change beliefs in isolation or without context. Rather, changing a belief means changing a part of a broader, complex, and adaptive belief system Scheffer et al., 2022; Homer-Dixon et al., 2013, which involves social and personal considerations, and in which belief systems and changes within them serve specific functions for individuals Oktar and Lombrozo, 2025; Dalege et al., 2025. This broader view also helps interpret one of our key findings. While most of our analyses have focused on the effect of external pressure on the belief itself, one of our key findings is that such pressure also changes how that belief is embedded in the individuals' belief networks. Compliance with the external pressure is associated with developing a more densely connected and less dissonant belief network. Resistance is also associated with developing a less dissonant belief network in which the focal belief takes a more central position than before. In other words, the external pressure does not only affect the focal belief, but it also leaves a trace in the belief network which will affect how beliefs can change in the future-a path dependency. For example, because belief networks become less dissonant and more connected after external pressure, future informational campaigns might be less effective than the first, resembling a backlash effect. Our explanation for this increasing resistance does not require invoking social conformity (Kahan et al., 2012), but relies on the internal adaptation of individual belief networks. ANB is related to the Learning Ising Attitude Model (LIAM; Smal et al., 2026), but the two models use different learning signals and address different questions. LIAM extends the network theory of attitudes (Dalege et al., 2016, 2018) by adding Hebbian learning to explain how balanced attitude networks can emerge and remain stable. In LIAM, relations are updated from covariation in node states: relations become positive when nodes repeatedly take the same state, negative when they take opposite states, and decay when no systematic covariation is present. This learning process is linked to attention, attitude importance, and disposition learning, which helps explain why attitude networks stabilize and why attitude change depends on attention and importance. ANB uses a related Hebbian logic, but relations are updated from belief changes: consistent belief change strengthens a relation, whereas inconsistent belief change weakens it. This difference matters because ANB focuses on how adaptive belief-network structures shape later responses to external pressure. Together with belief updating based on personal dissonance, social dissonance, and dissonance created by external pressure, internal adaptation can make belief networks grow apart, while social adaptation can make them more similar within social neighborhoods. Thus, LIAM and ANB are complementary: LIAM explains the emergence and stability of balanced attitude networks, whereas ANB explains heterogeneous compliance, resilience, and resistance under external pressure. While LIAM makes changing attention central, ANB keeps attention to dissonance fixed in the main simulations to isolate the effects of internal and social adaptation. The addition of social adaptation as a mechanism for belief network updating has interesting implications for the understanding of belief dynamics. Specifically, extreme social isolation provides a useful boundary case for social adaptation. Social adaptation assumes that belief relations are constrained by repeated interaction with social contacts. When such interaction is largely absent, there is less pressure for a person's belief network to converge toward relations shared in a social neighborhood. Ted Kaczynski, commonly known as 'Unabomber', a so-called 'lone wolf' terrorist in the US, who quit his academic career in 1970, isolated himself from society to live in the wilderness, developed a rather uncommon combination of radical environmentalism, anti-technology, anti-academia, and anti-left ideology and killed or injured 26 people through mail bombing over the next decades (Fleming, 2022). This case is not evidence for the typical development of belief networks. It illustrates why social constraints matter: when ordinary social contact is weak, unusual relations among beliefs may face fewer corrective or convergent pressures. In most cases, belief networks become aligned at least within social circles, which can lead to shared relations between beliefs or issues across societies, or to different belief networks in different groups. Our work suggests that to understand belief dynamics, we must measure not only beliefs and changes in beliefs, but belief relations and their changes over time. This, however, is not an easy measurement task. Most existing studies rely on belief networks estimated based on inter-individual correlations between beliefs, which likely obscures variations in individual network sizes, contents, and structures (Dalege et al., 2025; Boutyline &amp; Vaisey, 2017; Turner-Zwinkels et al., 2021). Measuring individual belief networks is more involved, with methods relying on longitudinal measurement (Chambon et al., 2022, 2023), pairwise comparisons of beliefs (Brandt, 2022), or visual canvas approaches, sometimes coupled with unstructured interviews (Fenn et al., 2025; Abar et al., 2017; Poulsen et al., 2026). Allowing belief relations to adapt makes belief-network structure both a condition for belief updating and an outcome of belief updating. Many explanations of belief persistence start with what people believe, what information they receive, or why they may be motivated to accept or reject it. Static belief-network models already show that beliefs should not be studied in isolation. The additional point here is that belief relations can change through internal adaptation and social adaptation. As belief relations change, people can develop different belief networks even when they start from similar beliefs and similar network structures. External pressure can therefore produce compliance, resilience, or resistance not only because people hold different beliefs. It can also do so because belief systems adapt differently over time. Beliefs do not only change within belief networks; they also reshape the belief networks that determine what can change next.

## A Illustration of belief network edge updating

Let us illustrate the consequences of the internal edge weight updating, $\omega_{m,n} \leftarrow \omega_{m,n} + \epsilon \cdot \partial x_m \cdot \partial x_n$, in equation 3 with a fabricated example.

First, we consider aligned beliefs that change in a consistent way with the structure. Assume Alice has beliefs xm = 0.1 and xn = 0.4-she supports both issues m and n moderately-and she sees these issues as somewhat positively related ωm,n = 0.3. In other words, her beliefs are aligned with the edge between them, thus contributing to low dissonance D = -ωm,n · xm · xn = -0.3 · 0.1 · 0.4 = -0.012. Assume now that in the next time step her beliefs on both items increase to xm = 0.5 (∂xm = 0.4) and xn = 0.6 (∂xn = 0.2). This further decreases her dissonance D = -ωm,n · xm · xn = -0.3 · 0.5 · 0.6 = -0.09. Given that the beliefs are positively related, we call this a structure-consistent belief update. Structure-consistent updating leads to a strengthening of the edge weight: ωm,n = 0.3 + 1 · 0.4 · 0.2 = 0.38 (equation (3) with λ = 0, ϵ = 1). This further reduces Alice's dissonance D = -0.38 · 0.5 · 0.6 = -0.114. The same logic applies to other structureconsistent belief change scenarios: (i) when two initially negatively valued beliefs that are connected through a positive edge become more negative over time, or (ii) when initially opposite beliefs connected through a negative edge further diverge over time. When beliefs are already aligned, structure-consistent belief change strengthens the belief network, with stronger edge weights |ωm,n| and thus leads to lower levels of dissonance.

Next, we consider aligned beliefs but structure-inconsistent belief changes. Assume Alice's beliefs, xm = 0.1 and xn = 0.4, change in opposite directions-for example, to xm = -0.2 (∂xm = -0.3) and xn = 0.8 (∂xn = 0.4, with the belief on issue m flipping from approval to disapproval. In this case, dissonance spikes up from D = -0.012 to D = -0.3 · (-0.2) · 0.8 = +0.048. The edge weight ωm,n decreases to ωm,n = 0.3 + 1 · (-0.3) · 0.4 = 0.18. This updating of the edge weight between beliefs m and n mitigates Alice's feeling of dissonance D = -0.18 · (-0.2) · 0.6 = 0.0288. With structure-inconsistent belief change, the belief network weakens and |ωm,n| decreases. Alice will in the future change her beliefs on issues m and n more independently from each other.

Finally, we look at an example of initially misaligned beliefs and structure-consistent updating. In this case, edge updating can lead to less trivial outcomes. Consider that Alice's beliefs are xm = -0.2 and xn(t = 0) = 0.6, even though the beliefs are related with a positive edge ωm,n = 0.3. As such, Alice feels high dissonance from holding the two beliefs, D = 0.036. Now consider that the two beliefs change in the same direction to xm = -0.1 (∂xm = 0.1) while xn = 0.9 (∂xn = 0.3), then the relation strengthens with edge weight ωm,n = 0.3 + 1 · 0.1 · 0.3 = 0.33 even though the beliefs are still misaligned. At first glance, it seems counter-intuitive that Alice will connect two misaligned beliefs more with each other because this temporarily increases the degree of dissonance she feels. However, because the beliefs change in a consistent way (and if Alice projects that they will do so in the next time steps as well), belief network updating induces additional pressure for Alice to align the beliefs earlier and thus pressure to resolve their dissonance and gain more reassurance as soon as they are aligned.

## E Sensitivity Analysis of Response Type Frequency

Figure A8 shows sensitivity analyses of the response frequencies-compliant, resistant, resilient-when varying key model parameters in different simulation scenarios-static belief networks with low or high edge weights and adaptive belief networks. Here, we focus in particular on whether simultaneity of response patterns in a single simulation, i.e. response frequencies that are different from 100% and 0%, remains a robust feature of simulations with adaptive belief networks. This is the case.

We describe our more detailed insights in the following:

- Increasing the number of beliefs leads to more resistance/resilience. This is true for both static and adaptive belief networks. The reason for this is that higher number of beliefs also implies more potential belief network dissonance (Dnon-focal and Dfocal) in comparison to dissonance due to external pressure (or social influence). With adaptive belief networks, we get simultaneity of response patterns in any simulation regardless of the number of beliefs.
- Increasing the number of agents (while keeping the average number of social contacts per agent fixed at 10) has no important effect on the response frequencies. For adaptive belief networks, response frequencies vary less across simulations in large populations-this follows the law of large numbers.
- Low attention to dissonance increases noise and thus also leads to simultaneity of response patterns in static belief networks. High attention to noise increases the frequency of resilient agents, that is agents that return to the negative belief once the pressure is removed. With adaptive belief networks, we get simultaneity of response patterns in any simulation regardless of the attention to dissonance.
- A smaller number of social contacts induces higher frequency of resilient agents for simulations with static and weak belief networks. A high number of social contacts fosters resilience for simulations with static and strong belief networks or with adaptive belief networks. In general, however, the simultaneity of responses remains robust for adaptive belief network simulations.
- By design, the number of past belief changes has no effect on static belief networks. For adaptive belief networks, however, averaging belief trends over a large number of time steps (10 instead of 1 time step), has a big impact on response frequencies. This happens because, as the averaging window for belief change becomes larger, belief networks adapt less and less and thus simulations with adaptive belief networks in principle fall back to the case of static belief networks with low (initial) edge weights. In this case, agents will comply with the pressure.

Tables A6 and A7 show the performance metrics for the logistic regression model-Table A6 classifies noncompliants vs. compliants, Table A7 classifies resistants vs. resilients. For each external pressure strength s, we fitted a logistic regression on a selection of (standardized) properties of the agents capturing beliefs, social embeddedness and belief-network structure just before the external pressure event (averaged between t = 91 and 100): the number of social contacts, triadic balance in the belief network around the focal belief, connectedness of the focal belief in the belief network, weighted clustering of the belief network, betweenness centrality of the focal belief, and the agent's focal belief (control variable). Note, for s = 8 and s = 16 the proportions of resistant agents are too small for reasonable comparison with resilient agents. The table shows the following:

- Proportions give the share of individuals in each category, i.e. resistants/resilients vs. compliants in Table A6 and resistant vs. resilients in Table A7. Note, N is constant because we use the same 100 random seeds and the simulations differ only from t &gt; 100, when the external pressure event produces different responses dependent on its strength s.
- McFadden R2 measures overall model fit relative to a null intercept-only model.
- Tjur R2 is the difference in mean predicted probabilities between the two outcome groups, providing an intuitive measure of discrimination (0 = no discrimination, 1 = perfect separation).
- The likelihood-ratio test (χ2 , p) compares the full model (all predictors) against a null model containing only the control variable, i.e. the focal belief, to predict the agents' response pattern. This tests whether knowing the agents' belief-network properties and social embeddedness improves the prediction of their response to the external pressure event beyond just knowing their focal belief. It does in all simulation settings.
